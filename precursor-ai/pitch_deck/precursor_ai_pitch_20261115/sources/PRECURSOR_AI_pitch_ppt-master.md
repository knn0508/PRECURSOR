Most hillsides have no ground sensors, so that movement goes unseen. And radar satellite data does exist, but it is huge and technical, so decision makers rarely use it.

### Speaker Notes

Here is how it works. NISAR sees the ground move, and terrain and rain tell us where and when that movement matters. We acquire NISAR, elevation, rainfall, soil moisture and landslide history. InSAR turns two radar passes into ground displacement. Every layer is aligned to one common grid with its sources recorded. Then we analyze movement over time, slope, aspect and rainfall build-up. ### Speaker Notes

This rests on real NASA data. NISAR, the NASA and ISRO L-band radar, gives us ground displacement with a twelve day repeat. L-band radar sees through vegetation, so it can see the ground under forest slopes. NASADEM gives elevation, slope and aspect. GPM IMERG gives rainfall, and SMAP gives soil moisture. The NASA Global Landslide Catalog shows us where landslides have already happened. The line about the area covered, the number of scenes and the date range is a placeholder we will fill in before the pitch.
